## GPT-6

### Honest reaction: Yes, I recognise myself in this.

I’m organising a significant birthday or reunion, probably for 6–12 friends or family members. I want to arrange something special that everyone will enjoy, but I don’t want the responsibility of paying for everyone and then chasing people for money.

The statement speaks to that problem directly.

### What resonates

- The occasions: Big birthdays, anniversaries and reunions are exactly the situations I have in mind.
- The group size: Six to twelve feels realistic for the kind of gathering I’d organise.
- The experience: A chef’s table or private food tour sounds more appealing than simply booking a restaurant table.
- Individual payments: This is the strongest selling point. I don’t want to finance the celebration for everyone else.
- Enjoying the day as a guest: This captures something important. I want to organise the occasion without spending the entire day managing it.

### What gives me pause

**1. Is there enough choice?**

You’ve mentioned two food-related examples. Are these the main types of experiences available, or could I also find wine tastings, workshops, cultural activities and other distinctive things to do?

**2. What does the deadline mean in practice?**

If two friends haven’t paid by the deadline, what happens? Do they lose their places, or am I still responsible for the cost? This matters enormously.

**3. Is the organiser genuinely free of responsibility?**

I still have to choose the experience, coordinate the group and make sure everyone understands the arrangements. Paying separately solves a significant problem, but it doesn’t automatically make me a guest.

### My verdict

I feel this is aimed at me, rather than at a generic traveller. The occasions, group size and financial frustration are recognisable.

The strongest message is not really about booking an experience. It’s about being able to organise a celebration without becoming everyone’s unpaid event manager or bank.

I’d be interested enough to explore it, particularly if the experience selection is good and the payment arrangements are genuinely straightforward.

One improvement: I’d consider replacing “never covering a seat you didn’t choose to” with something more natural and immediately understandable:

“Everyone pays their own way, so you can organise the celebration without picking up the bill for the group.”

That is the benefit I’d remember.

## Claude

### In character: I'm organising my sister's 40th, ten of us, one Saturday in March

Do I recognise myself? Partly. "Big birthday" catches my eye, and "enjoying the day as a guest" really lands. I'm always the one with the spreadsheet, not the one having fun. But the statement reads like a product spec, not like my problem.

### What speaks to me

- "Enjoying the day as a guest." Yes. That's what I want and never get.
- "Vetted local host who welcomes groups." Reassuring. My biggest fear is turning up with ten people to a host who clearly didn't want us.
- "Each guest paying their own share." Useful, but only once I've worked out how it fits my situation.

### What makes me hesitate

- What about my sister? It's her birthday. She isn't paying, so the rest of us split her share. The statement says each guest pays their own, which doesn't fit my occasion. Can I cover her seat, or split it between everyone?
- Will my friends have to sign up for something? If my aunt has to download an app and create an account, I'm back to chasing, just in a different place.
- "By a set deadline" sounds like admin. I'm planning a celebration, not collecting rent. It makes me picture awkward reminder emails going to my friends in my name.
- "Never covering a seat you didn't choose to." I had to read it twice. Is it telling me someone might drop out and I'd lose money? Now I'm worried about a problem I hadn't thought of.
- The "memorable" part has gone. I'm not booking a food tour, I'm trying to give my sister a day she'll talk about for years. Nothing here speaks to that.
- "Anniversary or reunion" waters it down. My parents' anniversary is a completely different situation: we kids pay, and they're the guests. Lumping everything together makes it feel less like it's for me.

Verdict: I'd click on it, but I'm not convinced. It solves the money problem I already know about and raises two new worries I didn't have.

### Out of character: what this tells you

- The money mechanic is right, but the framing is cold. Lead with the outcome (a day everyone remembers, and you get to enjoy it), then make split payment the proof.
- The honoured guest is a real gap. Birthdays and anniversaries nearly always have someone who doesn't pay. "Split the birthday person's share across the group" is a must-have feature and a strong line in the message.
- Address guest friction early. If guests can pay without creating an account, say so. It's the next objection after money.
- Replace the double negative with the rule. "Anyone who hasn't paid by the deadline? You decide whether to cover them or free up the place."
- Narrow the launch message to one occasion. Big birthdays are the clearest fit. Anniversaries and reunions have different payment dynamics, so test them separately.

### A version to test with real organisers

Book a standout experience with a local host who loves groups, such as a private cooking class or food tour, for 6–12 friends. Everyone pays their own share (and can split the birthday person's), with no app to download and no chasing. You get to enjoy the day like everyone else.

Only use the "no app to download" line if it's true at launch.
