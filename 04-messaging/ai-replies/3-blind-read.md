## GPT-6: cold read of my chosen visual

### 1. Who is it built for?

Primarily, someone organising an experience for a group of friends, probably a birthday, hen or stag party, or another special occasion.

More specifically, it’s aimed at the person who takes responsibility for making the plans, chooses the activity, books it and then ends up chasing everyone else for money. The reference to eleven friends suggests groups of around 6–12 people.

It understands a very recognisable frustration: organising something enjoyable shouldn’t become an administrative exercise.

### 2. What does the product do?

Roamly Groups makes booking and coordinating a shared experience easier.

From what I can see, it offers three things:

- Group booking: Book one experience for 6–12 people with a vetted local host who has agreed to accommodate groups.
- Split payments: Share a link with everyone so each person pays their own share by an agreed deadline.
- Control over unpaid guests: If someone doesn’t pay, the organiser decides whether to cover their share or release their place.

Essentially, it removes the organiser from the business of collecting money and coordinating individual payments.

The proposition is less about discovering experiences and more about making it easier to get everyone committed to one.

### 3. What makes you trust it?

Three things stand out:

- Specificity: The 6–12 people limit and the three clearly explained steps make the proposition feel tangible rather than vague marketing.
- Credibility: The claim of 12,000+ vetted local hosts provides some reassurance that Roamly has an established network rather than being a new intermediary with no supply.
- Transparency and control: I particularly like the explanation of what happens when someone doesn’t pay. It addresses an awkward real-world problem rather than pretending everything will go smoothly.

The design also helps. The strong typography, restrained colour palette and straightforward layout make it feel reasonably professional and considered.

### Where the image leaves me unsure

There are a few things I’d want to understand before booking:

1. What exactly am I booking? I understand the group payment mechanism, but I don’t see examples of the experiences available. Is this food, workshops, outdoor activities or something else?
2. What does vetted mean? How are hosts checked, and what assurance do I have about the quality of the experience?
3. What happens financially? When is the booking confirmed? What happens if someone doesn’t pay, the host cancels or the organiser needs to change the date?
4. Is it genuinely easier? Does everyone simply pay through Roamly, or do they need to create an account and go through a booking process themselves?

### Overall impression

I understand the problem, the intended audience and the core functionality almost immediately.

The strongest part is the opening message. It captures an emotionally familiar frustration and makes me feel Roamly understands the organiser’s experience.

I also appreciate that the product isn’t presented as another discovery platform. It’s solving the less glamorous but genuinely painful part of organising a group activity.

My main reservation is that the image sells the payment coordination solution much more clearly than the experiences themselves. I understand why I’d use Roamly Groups, but I’m not yet convinced why I’d choose Roamly over another provider.

Verdict: Clear problem, credible proposition and good audience targeting. I’d be interested enough to investigate further, but I’d need to see the experiences and understand the booking protections before committing.

## Claude: cold read of my chosen visual

At feed speed, the yellow line is what grabbed me. "Spent weeks chasing eleven friends for their share" is a problem plenty of people have had, so I stopped scrolling.

### 1. Who it's for

- The organiser in a friendship group: the person who books the birthday, hen do or reunion and then ends up as the debt collector.
- Groups of 6–12, so it's for planned social occasions rather than everyday plans.
- Unsure: whether it's for travel or for days out in your own city. The name "Roamly" suggests travel, but "local host" could mean either. I also couldn't tell whether work team outings count, since the copy only mentions "friends".

### 2. What it does

- Roamly seems to be a marketplace of experiences run by local hosts, and "Groups" looks like a feature added on top.
- You book one experience for the whole group. Everyone gets a link and pays their own share before a deadline. If someone doesn't pay, you either cover them or release their place.
- So really it's a split-payment and commitment tool attached to experience booking.
- Unsure:
  - Does the organiser pay upfront or hold the booking with a deposit?
  - If a place is freed up, does everyone else's price go up, or does the group just get smaller?
  - What happens if fewer than six people pay?
  - What kinds of experiences are on offer, and what do they cost?
  - What does "sorts the last bit" mean? I had to read the bullets to work it out.

### 3. What makes me trust it

- The "12,000+ vetted hosts" line and the shield icon carry most of the trust signal. "Who's said yes to groups" also helps, because it suggests I won't turn up with 12 people to a host who was expecting four.
- "Your call" makes me feel the organiser stays in control, which matters more to me than any single feature.
- Honestly, the trust is moderate. The ad earns my attention through empathy more than it earns my confidence:
  - "Vetted" isn't explained.
  - There are no reviews, ratings or social proof from other organisers.
  - There's nothing on payment security, refunds or cancellation.
  - I don't recognise the brand, so "12,000+" doesn't mean much to me yet.

### Other things I wasn't sure about

- I read the 11 dashed circles as the friends who haven't paid. It's a clever touch, but easy to miss at a glance.
- I couldn't work out what the third icon (the fork/branch) means.
- There's no price, call to action or link. I know the problem and the mechanism, but not what to do next or what it costs.
- The middle section has a lot of empty white space, which makes the ad feel slightly unfinished when it's small in a feed.

Overall: the problem comes across clearly, and so does the mechanism once you read the bullets. What I'd add is one concrete trust proof, such as a rating, an organiser quote or a refund promise, plus a price cue.
