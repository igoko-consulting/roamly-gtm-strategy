## GPT-6

### CMO pressure test: Roamly Groups

Overall assessment: Strong problem framing, plausible segment choice, but the GTM strategy is getting ahead of the evidence.

You’ve identified a credible customer problem and a potentially attractive product-led growth loop. However, there are three things I’d challenge before approving this strategy:

1. Whether occasion groups are genuinely the best commercial segment.
2. Whether split payments solve the actual reason bookings fail.
3. Whether acquiring new customers is the right primary objective when the evidence comes from existing customers.

### 1. Your weakest input: Primary goal – Acquire

My confidence: 90%.

You’ve selected Acquire because guests are potentially new to Roamly and could subsequently become independent customers.

That’s an attractive growth narrative, but it’s not yet supported by evidence.

Consider what you actually know:

- Existing Roamly users are raising the problem.
- Existing users are trying to coordinate group bookings.
- Existing bookings are being lost through friction.
- Guests might subsequently become independent customers.

The first three are evidence of an opportunity to improve conversion and monetise existing demand. The fourth is a hypothesis.

You’re effectively prioritising an unproven acquisition benefit over a more immediate commercial opportunity.

My challenge: Why would I fund a new acquisition strategy before you’ve demonstrated that you can recover bookings from customers we already have?

I’d want to see:

- Recovered bookings from existing customers.
- Incremental revenue per organiser.
- Number of new accounts created through group invitations.
- Percentage of invited guests who subsequently book independently.

I’d distinguish between the immediate objective and the longer-term growth opportunity.

Recommendation: Consider Expand as the primary goal, with Acquire as a secondary benefit to validate.

This doesn’t mean abandoning PLG. It means being clearer about where the initial commercial value comes from.

### 2. Your biggest blind spot: You’re assuming the payment problem is the booking problem

My confidence: 95%.

This is the assumption I’d be most concerned about.

Your narrative is that organisers struggle to collect money, coordinate accounts and manage availability.

You’ve consequently designed a solution around:

- Coordinated booking.
- Split payments.
- Shared itineraries.

But have you established which of these problems actually causes the booking to fail?

Imagine the following:

Six friends want to book an experience.

The organiser finds something everyone likes. However:

- Two people cannot make the proposed date.
- One person thinks it’s too expensive.
- Another hasn’t confirmed.
- The organiser doesn’t want to commit their own money before everyone agrees.

Would split payments solve this?

Possibly not.

The real problem might be group decision-making and commitment, rather than payment processing.

That’s an important distinction because it changes the product proposition.

If the primary friction is payment, split payments are central.

If the primary friction is coordination, shared availability, provisional reservations and group confirmation might be more valuable.

If the primary friction is commitment, the ability to reserve a place without personally underwriting the entire booking could be the differentiator.

My recommendation: Don’t assume payment is the primary problem simply because payment is where the drop-off occurs.

Investigate the preceding steps in the customer journey.

I’d want to understand:

1. How many people typically participate in the decision?
2. At what point does the group lose momentum?
3. Who carries the financial risk?
4. What proportion of failed bookings are actually attributable to payment friction?

This is probably your most important discovery question.

### 3. What I’d cut or sharpen

| Area | CMO challenge | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Business problem | Too much emphasis on payment friction before validating the cause. | Frame around group booking friction, with payment as a suspected cause. |
| Success metrics | Too many outcomes, some dependent on others. | Prioritise booking conversion, recovered revenue and incremental group bookings. |
| Signals | Repeat booking at 3× industry average doesn’t demonstrate readiness for a new feature. | Keep as evidence of existing customer loyalty, not product demand. |
| Segmentation | Occasion groups are plausible, but seasonality and host suitability are significant risks. | Validate against friend groups before committing. |
| Market sizing | Market reports and surveys are useful but insufficient. | Prioritise Roamly’s own behavioural data and addressable host capacity. |
| Stakeholders | Good coverage. | Add sales only if corporate groups become a serious target. |
| Value proposition | Strong, but assumes everyone wants to pay independently. | Test whether payment, coordination or commitment is the strongest benefit. |
| GTM motion | PLG makes sense, but CLG is currently an assumption. | Treat community as an experimental acquisition channel, not an established growth engine. |
| Primary goal | Acquisition is based on future potential rather than demonstrated value. | Consider Expand first, Acquire second. |

### 4. The argument I’d expect from your CMO

I’d put this question to you:

“Why are we building a group travel product for occasion planners rather than simply fixing the booking experience for groups who already want to use Roamly?”

It’s deliberately uncomfortable.

Your answer needs to establish why this is more than a checkout improvement.

I’d expect something along these lines:

- Group bookings represent incremental demand, not simply a different payment method.
- The organiser provides a natural distribution point to reach multiple potential customers.
- Each successful booking creates an opportunity to introduce Roamly to several new travellers.
- The longer-term opportunity is to turn individual transactions into a group-led acquisition and retention mechanism.

That’s a credible strategic narrative.

But you still need to demonstrate that the initial segment has sufficient demand and that the product addresses its biggest barrier.

### 5. My recommended position

I wouldn’t throw away your original strategy. I’d sharpen the commercial sequencing.

| Decision | Your proposal | My recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | Payment and booking friction | Group booking friction, with payment as a hypothesis |
| Segment | Occasion groups | Occasion groups as the initial test segment |
| Motion | Hybrid PLG + CLG | PLG primary, community discovery experimental |
| Goal | Acquire | Expand initially, Acquire as a secondary outcome |
| Value proposition | Book together, pay separately | Coordinate commitment, booking and payment in one place |

Three things I’d defend

1. Occasion groups: They have a clear organiser, a fixed date and potentially acute coordination problems. That’s a reasonable beachhead segment, even if it needs validation.
2. PLG primary: The low transaction value and self-service nature of the product make a sales-led motion difficult to justify.
3. Long-term acquisition potential: Every successful group booking introduces Roamly to potential future customers. That’s a compelling growth mechanism, even if it isn’t yet a proven acquisition channel.

### Final CMO verdict

I’d approve further discovery, not yet approve the full GTM strategy.

The opportunity is credible, the segment is defensible and the motion is sensible.

But you’re currently treating three hypotheses as established facts:

- Payment friction is the primary cause of failed group bookings.
- Occasion groups are the most attractive initial segment.
- Group invitations will translate into incremental customer acquisition.

My priority would be to validate the first before investing heavily in the solution.

One final distinction worth keeping in mind for your course: a strong GTM strategy doesn’t need to eliminate uncertainty. It needs to make clear which assumptions you’re willing to take forward, why, and how you’ll test them.

## Claude

### Skeptical CMO review: Roamly Groups GTM

Verdict: The diagnosis is good. The goal is wrong, the core mechanism is untested, and the plan tries to cover too much. Here is where I would push.

### 1. Weakest input: Primary goal = Acquire

Your own problem statement says this is a conversion problem. "Roamly is losing bookings it already has the demand for" describes existing users dropping off at payment. That is activation and conversion, not acquisition.

- Why it matters: The goal decides your north star, your channels and how you'll be judged. Optimise for acquisition and you'll spend on hen-party communities and influencers while the leak in your existing funnel stays open.
- The acquisition story is the weakest link too. Guests are low-intent, one-off participants in someone else's purchase. The claim that "every booking puts Roamly in front of 5–11 guests, so the product does the converting" is an assumption, not a mechanism. Exposure is not conversion.
- Fix: Make the primary goal Convert (recover abandoned group demand). Make Acquire the secondary loop goal, measured as guest-to-own-account booking within 90 days.

Close runner-up: the signal "repeat booking at 3x industry average shows trust to try something new". This is a non sequitur. It proves solo loyalty, not appetite for group booking. Cut it, or reframe it as low switching risk for the existing base.

### 2. The assumption you're too close to see

You assume payment friction causes the drop-off. It may be commitment friction. The payment step is often where a group finds out who isn't really coming.

- If that's true, split payments don't remove the failure. They surface it earlier and make it visible, with 12 people watching.
- This exposes the question your plan never answers: what happens when guest 9 of 12 doesn't pay? Possible answers:
  - The booking lapses, which repeats today's failure inside your product.
  - The organiser covers the shortfall, which brings back today's chasing problem.
  - The host absorbs it, which hurts host trust and capacity.
  - Roamly underwrites it, which creates a risk and finance problem.
- Whoever carries that risk is the product. It is also the real promise in your value proposition. Payments, risk, legal and host ops aren't supporting stakeholders here. They are the critical path.

Test before you build. Take a sample of abandoned multi-account sessions and find out why they died. Did a guest fail to pay, did the date collapse, or did headcount shrink? If most were date or headcount problems, split payment is secondary and availability-syncing is the lead feature.

Secondary blind spot: segment evidence. Your signals come from current users, likely friend groups. Your target is hen, stag and milestone organisers. Do you have data showing those occasion groups are in the abandoned sessions, or is this a segment you find attractive? Prove the overlap or choose the segment the data actually shows.

### 3. What I'd cut or sharpen

**Cut**

- Corporate offsites: a different buyer, invoicing, a sales-led motion. Remove it entirely, not just deprioritise it.
- "Roamly becomes where you plan a trip together": positioning aspiration, not measurable success. It belongs in a vision deck.
- CLG as a launch motion: posting in hen and wedding Facebook groups is community marketing, not community-led growth. For v1, go pure PLG to existing users who show the failed-group pattern. Treat community as a phase-2 test with its own CAC hypothesis.
- The user survey as a sizing input: stated intent on trips planned in the next 12 months is the least reliable data you'll gather. Use it for messaging, not sizing.

**Sharpen**

- Lead the business case with one number: monthly GMV lost in abandoned multi-account sessions. That figure gets finance in the room. Market reports don't.
- Put numbers on success:
  - North star: group session completion rate, X% → Y%.
  - Loop metric: % of guests booking independently within 90 days.
  - Guardrails: solo conversion, solo ratings, and host cancellation rate within ±Z%.
- Value prop: cut it to the organiser's outcome, for example "Book once. Never chase anyone for money." Then make sure the product can keep that promise when someone doesn't pay. Otherwise it's a liability.
- Stakeholders: you've listed everyone. Name the single decision owner and the long pole, which is likely payments and legal on partial-payment liability and host terms on group cancellations.
- Address the LTV gap: occasion organisers book infrequently and seasonally, perhaps once a year. Your case therefore depends entirely on the guest loop, which is your weakest-evidenced claim. Either show early data that supports it, or justify the segment on recovered GMV alone.

One-line challenge to take back to the lab: "Prove the drop-off is about money, and decide who pays when someone doesn't, before you spend a penny on acquisition."
